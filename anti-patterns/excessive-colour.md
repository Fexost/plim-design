# Excessive colour

Supporting material for [`philosophy.md`](../philosophy/philosophy.md) §17 (Accent Overload), §10 (Attention), and §8.10 (Inclusion). It adds depth, not rules.

**Core question (§17):** where should attention actually go?

Covers accent overload, gradients, and colour used as the only carrier of meaning.

## Recognising it

- The accent colour on buttons, links, icons, headings, borders, badges, and backgrounds at once.
- Several saturated colours competing on one screen without distinct meanings.
- Gradients on backgrounds, buttons, text, and icon tiles.
- Status shown only by a coloured dot, border, or text colour.

## Why it happens

Colour is the quickest way to add energy and brand presence. Accent colours spread because each individual use seems reasonable. Gradients read as modern and premium in isolation.

## Why it matters

- **Attention.** Colour is a strong attention signal (§10). If the accent is everywhere, it no longer marks the primary action or the important state.
- **Meaning.** When colours don't map to consistent meanings, people can't learn what they signify (§8.11).
- **Legibility.** Gradients and saturated backgrounds often reduce text contrast.
- **Inclusion.** Colour-only status fails people with colour vision deficiencies and in poor viewing conditions (§8.10).

## When it is not a problem

- Brands whose identity is colourful, where colour is applied with consistent roles (§8.15).
- Data visualisation, where colour distinguishes series, with labels or patterns as a second channel.
- A gradient that is part of the brand mark or carries meaning, such as a range or intensity.
- Status colours used consistently alongside text or icons.

## Diagnosing

- List every use of the accent colour. Which ones mark the primary action or key state?
- Does each colour have one meaning across the product?
- Does any status rely on colour alone?
- Does text remain readable on every coloured or gradient surface, in every theme?

## Repair

1. Give each colour a role (brand, action, status, surface, data) and apply it by that role, so that the strongest signal still marks what needs attention now. For a colourful brand, this can mean more colour, used deliberately, rather than less.
2. Where brand colour and action or status colour compete, separate them: by role, by placement, or by intensity, using the product's own palette.
3. Keep a gradient where it is part of the brand or carries meaning; otherwise consider a solid colour role, imagery, or typography for what the gradient was trying to express.
4. Add text or an icon to every colour-coded status (a requirement where status is conveyed; see [`plim-accessibility`](../skills/plim-accessibility/SKILL.md)).

## Overcorrection

Removing colour entirely can erase brand character and make states harder to distinguish. Colour is valuable precisely when it is spent deliberately.
