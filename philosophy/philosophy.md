# Plim Design Philosophy

> **Design with intention.**
>
> Create interfaces that are clear in purpose, coherent in structure, respectful of people, adaptable to context, expressive in identity, accessible by default, and refined through craft.
>
> **Plim does not prescribe what your product should look like. It teaches you how to make your product look and feel intentional.**

---

# 1. What Is Plim Design?

Plim Design is a framework-agnostic philosophy for creating digital interfaces.

It is not a component library.

It is not a visual theme.

It is not a fixed aesthetic.

It is not a collection of universal tokens.

It is not a replacement for an existing design system.

Plim is a way of thinking.

It provides principles and decision-making frameworks for understanding, designing, improving and reviewing interfaces.

Plim can be applied to:

* Angular
* React
* Vue
* Svelte
* vanilla HTML and CSS
* Tailwind
* Bootstrap
* Material
* shadcn
* proprietary design systems
* legacy applications
* native applications
* dashboards
* enterprise software
* consumer products
* marketing sites
* developer tools
* AI interfaces

The implementation can change.

The philosophy remains.

---

# 2. The Central Thesis

## Interfaces should be intentional.

A good interface is not defined by whether it is:

* minimal
* colourful
* modern
* expressive
* playful
* dense
* luxurious
* technical
* restrained

Those are aesthetic characteristics.

Good design is defined by the quality of its decisions.

A significant design decision should have a reason.

Why is this here?

Why is it prominent?

Why does it look different?

Why is it grouped with these things?

Why does it behave this way?

Why does it move?

Why is this information hidden?

Why is this action emphasized?

Why is this surface elevated?

Why is this colour being used?

Why is this pattern repeated?

The answer does not need to be complicated.

But there should be an answer.

Plim calls this **intentionality**.

---

# 3. Beauty Is an Outcome

"Make it beautiful" is not a sufficient design instruction.

It is especially dangerous as an instruction to an AI.

Without further guidance, "beautiful" can become:

* gradients
* glass
* shadows
* excessive rounding
* oversized typography
* decorative icons
* floating cards
* animated backgrounds
* excessive colour
* visual effects

None of these are inherently wrong.

The problem is using them without purpose.

Plim therefore treats beauty as an **emergent property**.

Beauty can emerge from:

* clarity
* hierarchy
* proportion
* rhythm
* coherence
* restraint
* typography
* meaningful motion
* appropriate density
* accessibility
* responsiveness
* interaction quality
* character
* craft

A beautiful interface may be visually quiet.

It may also be highly expressive.

The aesthetic can change.

The quality of the decisions should not.

> **Beauty is the felt quality of coherent, purposeful and carefully crafted decisions.**

---

# 4. What Plim Learns From Apple

Apple's Human Interface Guidelines provide a particularly strong foundation around purpose, agency, responsibility, familiarity, flexibility, simplicity, craft and delight. Apple's current guidance explicitly describes these principles as tools for weighing competing priorities rather than as a rigid formula.

Plim adopts these underlying ideas without attempting to reproduce Apple's visual language.

### Purpose

Design should create meaningful value.

### Agency

People should remain in control and be able to recover from mistakes.

### Responsibility

Products should respect privacy, safety, transparency and trust.

### Familiarity

Established patterns reduce unnecessary learning.

### Flexibility

Interfaces should adapt to diverse contexts, people and input methods.

### Simplicity

Remove unnecessary friction, not necessary capability.

### Craft

Quality emerges through attention to details and iteration.

### Delight

Emotional quality should emerge from a thoughtful experience rather than decorative additions.

These principles are not rules to maximize independently.

They are forces that must be balanced.

---

# 5. What Plim Learns From Material

Material's greatest contribution to Plim is its systemic approach.

Material demonstrates that:

* colour
* typography
* shape
* spacing
* elevation
* motion
* components
* states
* themes
* layouts

can form a coherent design language rather than a collection of isolated decisions.

Material 3 describes itself as an adaptable design system, and its current direction emphasizes expressive and adaptive products.

Material 3 Expressive provides another important lesson.

Google's research found that expressive treatments such as colour, size, shape, motion and containment can communicate emotion while also improving usability by emphasizing important actions and grouping related elements. The research covered 46 studies and more than 18,000 participants.

Plim therefore adopts the deeper lesson:

> **Expression and usability are not opposites. Expression becomes valuable when it communicates something.**

Plim does not reproduce Material's visual language.

It adopts its systemic thinking.

# 6. What Plim Adds

Apple and Material provide powerful foundations.

Plim exists because there is another problem:

> **How should design principles become useful reasoning for humans and AI working on real interfaces?**

Plim therefore emphasizes several ideas that are particularly important to AI-assisted design:

* intentionality
* semantic honesty
* attention as a resource
* designing for reality rather than screenshots
* improvement before replacement
* preserving existing systems
* distinguishing novelty from improvement
* explaining significant decisions
* preserving human control
* designing the edges
* balancing principles rather than maximizing them

These ideas form the bridge between design philosophy and design intelligence.

# 7. The Eight Plim Laws

The laws are the deepest layer of Plim.

They should remain true regardless of framework, visual style, product category or technology.

---

## Law 1 — Intent Before Appearance

Understand what something is for before deciding how it should look.

Before changing an interface, understand:

* its purpose
* its users
* their tasks
* the desired outcomes
* the surrounding context

Do not begin with:

> "How can this look better?"

Begin with:

> "What should this help someone understand or accomplish?"

---

## Law 2 — Meaning Before Decoration

Visual decisions should communicate meaning.

Colour, size, shape, spacing, elevation, typography, imagery and motion can communicate:

* hierarchy
* grouping
* state
* affordance
* importance
* context
* status
* identity

If a visual treatment communicates nothing meaningful, question whether it is necessary.

---

## Law 3 — Clarity Before Complexity

The interface should make important things understandable.

People should be able to determine, with appropriate effort:

* where they are
* what matters
* what they can do
* what is happening
* what happened
* what happens next
* how to recover

Clarity does not mean minimalism.

It means understandable organization.

---

## Law 4 — Coherence Without Uniformity

The interface should feel like one system.

But coherence does not mean identical treatment.

Similar things should feel related.

Different things should be distinguishable.

Consistency should exist primarily through relationships and principles, not arbitrary identical values.

---

## Law 5 — Respect the Person

Interfaces should preserve:

* agency
* attention
* time
* privacy
* accessibility
* dignity
* trust

The system should help people accomplish their goals without unnecessary friction, manipulation or confusion.

---

## Law 6 — Design for Reality

The interface is not the screenshot.

Design for:

* real data
* real devices
* real input
* real users
* errors
* latency
* empty states
* long content
* localization
* accessibility settings
* changing viewports
* repeated use
* interruptions
* edge cases

The happy path is only one state of the product.

---

## Law 7 — Improve Before Replacing

Understand existing systems before replacing them.

Preserve valuable:

* functionality
* conventions
* design language
* components
* tokens
* architecture
* brand identity
* accessibility behaviour
* user expectations

Change what needs changing.

Do not redesign merely to demonstrate change.

---

## Law 8 — Human Judgment Remains Central

AI can:

* analyze
* critique
* propose
* implement
* test
* refine

But it should not become the unquestionable authority over:

* product purpose
* brand identity
* audience
* strategy
* emotional character
* important trade-offs

AI should amplify design judgment.

It should not erase it.

---

# 8. The Sixteen Plim Principles

The laws describe what Plim believes.

The principles describe how those beliefs manifest in design.

---

## 8.1 Purpose

Design for meaningful outcomes.

Ask:

* Who is this for?
* What are they trying to accomplish?
* Why does this experience exist?
* What outcome matters?
* What should the interface make easier?

Purpose is the first filter.

---

## 8.2 Clarity

Make the important understandable.

Clarity comes from:

* hierarchy
* grouping
* typography
* spacing
* contrast
* language
* familiar patterns
* predictable behaviour
* feedback

A sparse interface can be unclear.

A dense interface can be clear.

The goal is not fewer things.

The goal is easier understanding.

---

## 8.3 Agency

People should be able to act intentionally.

Support:

* choice
* exploration
* cancellation
* undo
* recovery
* understandable consequences

Avoid:

* unnecessary forced flows
* deceptive interaction
* unexplained automation
* irreversible actions without adequate warning
* unnecessary lock-in

---

## 8.4 Familiarity

Use established knowledge as leverage.

People arrive with expectations about:

* buttons
* links
* navigation
* forms
* menus
* dialogs
* selection
* feedback

Reuse familiar conventions when they reduce cognitive effort.

Introduce novelty when it provides meaningful value.

---

## 8.5 Coherence

Design decisions should reinforce one another.

Coherence can exist across:

* typography
* spacing
* colour
* shape
* components
* interaction
* motion
* language
* imagery

Coherence should make the product feel intentional.

---

## 8.6 Hierarchy

Not everything deserves equal attention.

Hierarchy can be created through:

* position
* scale
* typography
* contrast
* spacing
* colour
* grouping
* containment
* motion

Hierarchy should correspond to importance.

If everything is emphasized, nothing is emphasized.

---

## 8.7 Restraint

Every element should earn its presence.

Question unnecessary:

* decoration
* visual emphasis
* colour
* motion
* controls
* containers
* repetition
* complexity

But restraint does not mean removing things indiscriminately.

> **Remove unnecessary complexity, not necessary capability.**

---

## 8.8 Rhythm

Interfaces should have an underlying visual rhythm.

Rhythm emerges through:

* spacing
* alignment
* typography
* repetition
* density
* section structure

Good rhythm makes an interface feel organized before the user consciously analyzes it.

---

## 8.9 Adaptability

The interface should remain appropriate as context changes.

Consider:

* viewport
* device
* orientation
* input method
* content length
* language
* accessibility preferences
* expertise
* task complexity

Responsive design is therefore contextual adaptation, not merely breakpoint management.

---

## 8.10 Inclusion

Accessibility belongs inside design rather than after it.

WCAG establishes four foundational dimensions:

* perceivable
* operable
* understandable
* robust

Plim uses these as a technical foundation while asking a broader question:

> Can people comfortably understand and use this interface?

Accessibility should influence:

* hierarchy
* contrast
* typography
* focus
* keyboard interaction
* target sizes
* motion
* language
* semantics
* errors
* component behaviour

from the beginning.

---

## 8.11 Semantic Honesty

Visual language should truthfully communicate functional relationships.

If something looks interactive, it should behave interactively.

If two things appear grouped, there should be a meaningful relationship.

If something appears important, its importance should be real.

If something is decorative, it should not masquerade as information.

> **The visual model should help people build an accurate mental model of the product.**

---

## 8.12 Appropriate Complexity

Complexity is not inherently bad.

Professional software may need:

* dense information
* advanced controls
* powerful configuration
* complex workflows

The goal is to eliminate **unnecessary** complexity.

Use progressive disclosure where appropriate.

Expose advanced capability when it becomes relevant.

Do not simplify a product by removing capability that users genuinely need.

---

## 8.13 Confidence

Good interfaces reduce uncertainty.

Users should understand:

* what they are doing
* what the system is doing
* whether something succeeded
* what happens next
* what went wrong
* how to recover

The goal is not merely task completion.

> **The goal is confident action.**

---

## 8.14 Continuity

The user's mental model should survive change.

Transitions, navigation, loading, responsive changes and state changes should preserve meaningful relationships.

Motion can communicate:

* what changed
* where something came from
* where something went
* what deserves attention

Motion should explain change rather than decorate it.

---

## 8.15 Character

Products should be allowed to have an identity.

Character can emerge through:

* typography
* colour
* shape
* imagery
* density
* motion
* language
* composition
* interaction style

Plim does not prescribe the character.

It provides the discipline through which character can emerge intentionally.

---

## 8.16 Craft

Craft is the discipline of finishing.

Craft appears in:

* alignment
* spacing
* typography
* language
* iconography
* state design
* transitions
* responsiveness
* performance
* accessibility
* edge cases

Craft is not visual perfectionism.

It is care applied consistently.

---

# 9. The Fundamental Design Tensions

Plim does not believe principles should be maximized independently.

Good design requires trade-offs.

---

## Simplicity ↔ Capability

Too little:

> The interface is clean but cannot support real work.

Too much:

> Everything is exposed at once.

Ask:

> What complexity is necessary for this user and task?

---

## Familiarity ↔ Identity

Too familiar:

> The product becomes generic.

Too novel:

> The product becomes difficult to understand.

Ask:

> Where should convention dominate, and where should identity emerge?

---

## Consistency ↔ Context

Too consistent:

> Everything behaves the same even when it shouldn't.

Too contextual:

> Nothing feels predictable.

Ask:

> What relationships should remain stable, and what should adapt?

---

## Restraint ↔ Expression

Too restrained:

> The interface loses character.

Too expressive:

> The interface competes with its own purpose.

Ask:

> What level of expression supports the product and its users?

Material 3 Expressive's research reinforces this tension: expressive treatments can improve attention and preference, while Google also explicitly notes that some users prefer calmer experiences.

---

## Density ↔ Breathing Room

Too sparse:

> The interface becomes inefficient.

Too dense:

> The interface becomes overwhelming.

Ask:

> What density matches the task, expertise and frequency of use?

---

## Automation ↔ Agency

Too little automation:

> The product creates unnecessary work.

Too much automation:

> The user loses understanding or control.

Ask:

> What should the system do automatically, and where should the person remain in control?

---

## Innovation ↔ Familiarity

Innovation can create differentiation.

Familiarity can create immediate understanding.

Neither is inherently superior.

Ask:

> Does the novelty create meaningful value?

---

# 10. Attention Is a Design Resource

Every interface has a finite attention budget.

Attention can be demanded through:

* colour
* contrast
* size
* position
* whitespace
* motion
* elevation
* imagery
* typography

Use these deliberately.

If every element receives strong emphasis, the interface loses hierarchy.

A useful design question is:

> **What deserves attention right now?**

Then:

> **What can afford to be quiet?**

This is one of the most important protections against generic AI-generated UI.

---

# 11. Visual Distinctions Should Mean Something

Whenever a design introduces a:

* border
* colour
* background
* shadow
* divider
* larger heading
* different weight
* animation
* container

ask:

> **What distinction is this communicating?**

Possible answers include:

* importance
* grouping
* state
* ownership
* interaction
* hierarchy
* context
* status
* identity

"Because it looks nicer" is not necessarily sufficient.

Visual language should carry meaning.

---

# 12. The Interface Is a System of States

An interface is not a collection of screenshots.

It is a system of:

* states
* transitions
* actions
* feedback
* relationships

Meaningful components should be considered across relevant states, including:

* default
* hover
* focus
* active
* disabled
* loading
* success
* error
* empty
* selected
* partial
* offline
* recovery

Not every component requires every state.

But every meaningful state should be intentional.

---

# 13. Design the Edges

The happy path is only one possible state.

Real products encounter:

* long labels
* missing content
* unexpected content
* localization
* slow networks
* failed requests
* permissions
* empty collections
* large collections
* keyboard navigation
* zoom
* reduced motion
* assistive technologies
* interrupted workflows

A polished interface survives these conditions.

> **Design the edges, not just the centre.**

---

# 14. Existing Design Systems

Plim is designed to operate alongside existing systems.

If a product uses Material, Bootstrap, Tailwind, shadcn, Carbon, Polaris, Primer or a proprietary system, Plim should first understand the system rather than attempting to replace it.

Existing design systems already encode knowledge about:

* components
* patterns
* accessibility
* interaction
* tokens
* brand
* implementation constraints

Atlassian, Primer and Carbon all emphasize some combination of cohesion, accessibility, consistency, modularity and reusable foundations.

Plim therefore follows:

> **Understand → preserve → improve → extend → replace only when justified.**

Plim is a reasoning layer, not another skin.

---

# 15. Identity Without Imposition

Plim should never make every product look like Plim.

That would contradict the philosophy.

A product's visual identity should emerge from:

* purpose
* audience
* brand
* context
* content
* emotional intent

The resulting interface may be:

* quiet
* expressive
* editorial
* technical
* playful
* luxurious
* utilitarian
* experimental

All can be Plim.

The philosophy remains stable.

> **Plim should look intentional without telling you what your product should look like.**

---

# 16. Against Trend-Driven Design

Trends are not inherently bad.

But they should never become automatic answers.

Plim should question default use of:

* glassmorphism
* gradients
* excessive rounding
* excessive shadows
* pill-shaped everything
* giant hero sections
* card-heavy layouts
* bento grids
* decorative blobs
* excessive animation
* 3D decoration
* accent-colour overload
* generic AI aesthetics

None are forbidden.

The question is:

> **What does this accomplish?**

If the answer is meaningful, use it.

If the answer is merely "it looks modern," reconsider.

---

# 17. Common AI Interface Failure Modes

AI systems have a tendency to converge on recognizable visual patterns.

Plim should actively guard against:

### Card Soup

Everything becomes a card.

Ask:

> Are these actually independent surfaces?

---

### Decoration Inflation

Every area receives gradients, shadows, icons and effects.

Ask:

> What meaning does each treatment communicate?

---

### Uniform Rounding

Everything gets the same radius.

Ask:

> Which things should actually belong to the same shape language?

---

### Accent Overload

The primary colour appears everywhere.

Ask:

> Where should attention actually go?

---

### Typography Inflation

Large headings are used to create drama.

Ask:

> Does the visual hierarchy represent actual importance?

---

### Dashboard Syndrome

Every page becomes a grid of cards, metrics and charts.

Ask:

> What is the user's actual task?

---

### Screenshot Optimization

The interface looks excellent in a static frame but fails in real use.

Ask:

> What happens when the content, viewport, state or interaction changes?

---

### Trend Substitution

"Modern" becomes a substitute for reasoning.

Ask:

> What problem does this design decision solve?

---

### Unnecessary Redesign

A working pattern is replaced because the agent prefers another pattern.

Ask:

> What meaningful improvement justifies this change?

---

# 18. Design for AI

Plim is intentionally designed for a world where AI participates in interface design and implementation.

That requires additional discipline.

---

## Understand Before Changing

An AI agent should understand:

* product purpose
* users
* tasks
* current design language
* existing components
* existing tokens
* constraints
* accessibility
* brand
* technical environment

before making substantial changes.

---

## Diagnose Before Decorating

The agent should identify the actual problem before selecting a visual treatment.

Do not begin with:

> "How can I make this more premium?"

Begin with:

> "What is preventing this experience from being clear, coherent, appropriate or satisfying?"

---

## Preserve Before Replacing

The agent should identify what already works.

Sometimes the correct change is:

> **No change.**

---

## Distinguish Change From Improvement

A change is not automatically an improvement.

New is not automatically better.

Different is not automatically better.

More sophisticated is not automatically better.

More visually impressive is not automatically better.

> **Every significant change should improve something that matters.**

---

## Explain Significant Decisions

For meaningful changes, an AI agent should be able to communicate:

* what changed
* why it changed
* which principle motivated it
* what trade-off was considered
* what was deliberately preserved

---

## Preserve Human Control

AI-assisted design should be:

* understandable
* reviewable
* incremental
* reversible

The more consequential the change, the more important human review becomes.

---

## Surface Uncertainty

When product intent, brand direction or user needs are unclear, the agent should not invent certainty.

It should identify the ambiguity.

Good design intelligence knows when it does not know enough.

---

# 19. The Plim Design Decision Model

When making a meaningful design decision:

### 1. Understand

What is the product?

Who is using it?

What are they trying to accomplish?

---

### 2. Identify Intent

What should this experience help the person understand or do?

---

### 3. Establish Priority

What matters most?

What deserves attention?

What can be secondary?

---

### 4. Understand Context

Consider:

* device
* viewport
* input
* accessibility
* content
* expertise
* frequency
* brand
* existing design system
* technical constraints

---

### 5. Preserve Useful Conventions

What should remain familiar?

---

### 6. Identify the Tension

What competing goals must be balanced?

---

### 7. Choose the Simplest Effective Solution

Do not add complexity unless it solves a meaningful problem.

---

### 8. Establish the System

Make the decision coherent with the surrounding experience.

---

### 9. Design the States

Consider relevant:

* default
* interaction
* focus
* loading
* success
* error
* empty
* disabled
* responsive

states.

---

### 10. Verify Inclusion

Check whether the design remains:

* perceivable
* operable
* understandable
* robust

and whether it provides an appropriately usable experience.

---

### 11. Test the Edges

Consider:

* unexpected content
* long content
* missing content
* localization
* small screens
* large screens
* keyboard
* zoom
* reduced motion
* assistive technologies
* failure

---

### 12. Polish

Only after structural problems are resolved should visual refinement become the primary concern.

---

### 13. Review

Ask:

> **Did this actually improve the experience?**

Not:

> **Did this make the interface look different?**

---

# 20. The Plim Beautification Model

"Beautify" does not mean decorate.

A Plim beautification process should generally follow:

```text
UNDERSTAND
    ↓
AUDIT
    ↓
IDENTIFY INTENT
    ↓
ESTABLISH HIERARCHY
    ↓
IMPROVE COMPOSITION
    ↓
REFINE FOUNDATIONS
    ↓
REFINE COMPONENTS
    ↓
IMPROVE STATES
    ↓
IMPROVE RESPONSIVENESS
    ↓
ADD MEANINGFUL MOTION
    ↓
VERIFY ACCESSIBILITY
    ↓
POLISH
    ↓
REVIEW
```

The sequence is important.

Do not polish a broken hierarchy.

Do not animate a confusing interaction.

Do not introduce a new colour system when the actual problem is information architecture.

Do not replace components when composition would solve the problem.

# 21. Definitions

Plim uses several terms deliberately.

## Simple

Not "few."

Simple means:

> **Easy to understand and accomplish without unnecessary friction.**

---

## Minimal

A visual aesthetic characterized by reduction.

Minimalism is neither inherently good nor bad.

---

## Modern

Not a visual trend.

A modern interface is one that appropriately responds to contemporary users, technologies, contexts and expectations.

---

## Premium

Not more decoration.

Premium means:

* considered
* coherent
* precise
* trustworthy
* polished
* appropriate

---

## Expressive

Design that communicates identity, emotion or emphasis through visual and interactive language.

Expression should reinforce purpose.

---

## Consistent

Similar things behave and communicate similarly.

Consistency does not mean identical appearance.

---

## Polished

An interface whose avoidable rough edges have been addressed across visuals, behaviour, states, accessibility and edge cases.

---

## Delight

A positive emotional quality emerging from thoughtful interaction.

Delight should support the experience rather than compete with it.

---

## Beautiful

An interface whose decisions feel coherent, purposeful and carefully crafted.

---

# 22. Accessibility Is Part of Design Quality

Plim treats accessibility as foundational.

WCAG provides the technical baseline through its principles of perceivable, operable, understandable and robust content.

Plim adds a design question:

> **Does this experience make sense to people with different abilities, contexts and ways of interacting?**

Accessibility should influence design from the beginning.

Not:

```text
design → build → accessibility audit
```

but:

```text
understand → design inclusively → build → verify
```

Accessibility often improves the experience for everyone because clear hierarchy, predictable interaction, meaningful feedback and understandable language are broadly valuable.

---

# 23. Design for Confidence

A strong interface does more than allow completion.

It creates confidence.

The person should feel:

> I understand what I'm looking at.

> I understand what I can do.

> I understand what will happen.

> I know whether it worked.

> I know what went wrong.

> I know how to recover.

Confidence emerges from:

* clarity
* feedback
* familiarity
* agency
* predictable states
* appropriate hierarchy
* meaningful error handling

> **The goal of clarity is confident action.**

# 24. The Plim Standard

Before considering an interface finished, ask:

### Purpose

* Is the purpose clear?
* Does the design serve it?

### Clarity

* Is hierarchy understandable?
* Can people identify what matters?

### Agency

* Can people control their actions?
* Can they recover from mistakes?

### Familiarity

* Are established conventions used where appropriate?
* Is novelty justified?

### Coherence

* Does the interface feel like one system?
* Do visual and interaction decisions reinforce each other?

### Identity

* Does it feel appropriate to this product?
* Does it have character without compromising usability?

### Attention

* Is emphasis being spent deliberately?
* Is anything unnecessarily demanding attention?

### Adaptability

* Does it respond appropriately to different contexts?

### Inclusion

* Can people perceive, operate and understand it?
* Does it remain robust?

### States

* Have meaningful states been considered?

### Edges

* What happens when reality deviates from the happy path?

### Restraint

* Is anything present without sufficient purpose?

### Craft

* Have the details been finished?

### Confidence

* Does the experience reduce uncertainty?

### Delight

* Does it feel appropriate and satisfying?

### Final question

> **Do the important decisions feel intentional?**

# 25. The Relationship Between Plim Design, Skills and Components

Plim has three layers.

## Plim Design

The philosophy.

It defines:

* beliefs
* laws
* principles
* tensions
* vocabulary
* decision-making

---

## Plim Design Skills

The operational intelligence.

Skills translate the philosophy into behaviour for AI agents.

They may include:

* design
* beautification
* review
* accessibility
* responsive design
* interaction
* existing-design-system integration
* visual refinement
* `plim-ui` integration

Skills should derive from the philosophy.

They should not create competing philosophies.

---

## Plim UI

The implementation.

`plim-ui` is an Angular component library demonstrating how Plim principles can be implemented in an accessible, composable component system.

It is a reference implementation.

It is not the definition of Plim.

Plim Design can exist without `plim-ui`.

`plim-ui` becomes stronger because it is informed by Plim Design.

# 28. Plim in One Paragraph

Plim Design is a framework-agnostic philosophy for creating intentional digital interfaces. Inspired by Apple's human-centered principles and Material's systemic and expressive approach, Plim focuses on the quality and reasoning behind design decisions rather than prescribing a visual style. It treats purpose, clarity, agency, familiarity, coherence, hierarchy, restraint, adaptability, inclusion, semantic honesty, confidence, character and craft as interconnected principles that must be balanced according to context. Plim rejects decoration for its own sake, trend-driven design, screenshot optimization and unnecessary reinvention. It is designed to work with existing products and design systems while providing a reasoning layer that helps humans and AI make better interface decisions. Its goal is not to make every product look like Plim. Its goal is to help every product look and feel intentional.
